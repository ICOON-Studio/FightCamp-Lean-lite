# CARDBOX1 - Three real boxing bouts broken down, and two ways to play one round as card against card

A paper, no engine code. One sport, boxing, two boxers. No mixed arts, no ground, no kicks. No dice as the main resolver: the game shows what will happen before a card is played. No copied text from any reference game: every idea below is in our own words, with the game it comes from named.

## How to read this page

- **Why.** The owner played the card game prototype (`proto/cardgame/`, boxing v Brazilian jiu-jitsu) and said the card game does not make sense, to break down a real fight and make it work with card logic, and that the real test should be boxer v boxer, not mixing classes until all arts cards are in game. This page is the notebook and the table: three real bouts written down round by round, then one real round replayed by hand as cards laid by both men, two different ways, then a pick.
- **What exists today, measured.** Over 400 bot bouts (`node proto/cardgame/mirror.mjs`, boxer v boxer): every bout ends by knockout (400 of 400); the man who goes second wins 69.5%; the combination rule fires 0.02 times a bout; the body hook 0.10 times; 81% of turns are spent at punching range; 68 decisions a bout; both men end near zero health. It is a race to zero, not a boxing match. The boxing cards today (`cards.mjs`, `N`): Jab, Cross, Lead hook, Body hook, Uppercut, Slip, Block, plus Sprawl and Escape (3 of 15 cards, never used boxer v boxer, not boxing). The turn today (`engine.mjs`): a man answers the other's volley with one answer, it resolves in order, then lays his own volley face up. This page does not patch those files. It decides how the boxing card game is rebuilt.
- **Method.** A designer would watch three fights with a notebook, write what decided each round, and only then deal cards on a table against a friend and replay one round by hand. Part 1 is the notebook. Part 2 is the list of things that decide real bouts. Part 3 is the table: one real round walked through two different turn structures, exchange by exchange. Part 4 is the pick and the deck.
- **(UNSURE).** Every statement the writer is not sure of is marked (UNSURE). An invented punch count is worse than none: CompuBox-style numbers are given only where the writer is sure of them, with the source named. A second seat will check facts against sources. Where a round's exact punch is not sure, the page says so and describes only the general pattern that is sure.
- **Sources named.** For each bout: date, venue, titles, result, official scorecards, CompuBox totals where sure, and the reports the round notes come from (named in each bout's source line). No source text is copied.
- **Contract.** Owns: `proto/cardgame/CARDBOX1_BREAKDOWN.md`. No other file. No code. Regression: `proto/cardgame` unchanged.

### 1A. Out-boxer against pressure fighter: Lennox Lewis v David Tua

**The bout.** 11 November 2000, Mandalay Bay, Las Vegas, Nevada, USA. WBC, IBF and IBO heavyweight titles. Lennox Lewis (orthodox, 6 ft 5 in) v David Tua (orthodox, 5 ft 10 in). Referee: Joe Cortez (UNSURE on referee name - reported as Cortez in contemporary reports, second seat to confirm). Result: Lewis wins by 12-round unanimous decision. Official scorecards: 119-109 (Dave Moretti), 118-110 (Chuck Giampa), 117-111 (Jerry Roth), all for Lewis. No knockdowns (UNSURE - no knockdown is reported in the reports read, second seat to confirm). CompuBox (sure, via ESPN and contemporary reports): Lewis landed 300 punches to Tua's 110; of Lewis's landed punches, 213 were jabs; Lewis threw 261 more punches than Tua and outlanded him at almost 3-to-1. Lewis's strategy, in the reports' words: circle and jab, throw right-hand leads. Tua's plan: get under the jab and land the left hook. Tua came in with a two-month-old rib cartilage injury, aggravated in round 2 by a Lewis body shot (ruled a low blow by the referee, harshly in one report's view). Tua's corner wet his hair down after round 5 to keep it out of his eyes (UNSURE - reported in one contemporary report, second seat to confirm).

**The styles.** Lewis is the out-boxer: taller, longer reach, fights at long range, circles, jabs to blind and measure, throws the straight right behind the jab, ties up or spins out when the shorter man gets inside. Tua is the pressure fighter: short, compact, walks forward, wants to get under the jab, work the body, and land the left hook to the head. The whole fight is whether Tua can get inside. He cannot.

**What this bout teaches.** Range is decided by the jab. The out-boxer does not need power to win; he needs to keep the pressure man at the end of the jab for 12 rounds. Body work paid once (round 2, the rib) and then never again - but that once restricted Tua's movement for 10 rounds. Clinches are the out-boxer's escape when the pressure man corners him: tie up or spin out, referee breaks, back to long range. Judges score it a shutout because the jab scores every round and the pressure man lands almost nothing clean. A card game that cannot score the jab, cannot fight for range, and cannot tie up to reset range cannot play this fight.

The bell matters: the fight is 12 rounds, and the last three are scored like the first nine.

## Part 2 - The parts of a boxing bout a card game must carry

From Part 1, the list of things that decide real bouts. Each entry gives: what it is, how often it matters (the writer's estimate, marked as such), and what a player would DECIDE about it. Estimates are the writer's, not measured.

1. **Range and who sets it.** What it is: where the fight is fought - long range (jab range), punching range (power punches), inside (the clinch), on the ropes, in the corner. The man who sets the range decides which punches can land. How often it matters: every exchange (estimate - range decides every punch). What the player decides: whether to step in, step out, circle, cut the ring, tie up, or spin out of the corner - and which punches are legal at the range he chooses.
2. **The jab as a tool (score, blind, measure, set up).** What it is: the lead jab scores, blinds the other man, measures the distance, and sets up the straight right and the hook. Lewis landed 213 jabs of 300 punches; De La Hoya landed 143 jabs of 263. How often it matters: every round (estimate - the jab decides the range and scores in every round of all three bouts). What the player decides: whether to jab to score, to blind, to measure, or to set up - and whether to throw the jab as a feint (looks like a jab, does nothing, but draws a defence).
3. **The lead against the counter.** What it is: the lead punch (jab, straight right, hook) against the counter (the punch thrown as the other man leads). Klitschko's overhand right in round 6 is a counter to Joshua's aggression; De La Hoya's counter right in round 5 exploits Trinidad's forward movement. How often it matters: most rounds (estimate - at least one lead-or-counter moment decides most rounds). What the player decides: whether to lead (throw first, risk the counter) or to counter (wait, risk being outscored), and which counter to throw.
4. **Combinations (why a second punch lands when the first is seen).** What it is: two or three punches thrown together. The first punch is seen; the second lands because the defender has used his defence on the first, or is blinded by the jab, or is moving the wrong way. Lewis's 3-4-punch combinations from round 7; De La Hoya's three-shot combinations in rounds 5 and 8; Joshua's barrage in round 5. How often it matters: most rounds (estimate - combinations score in most rounds of all three bouts). What the player decides: whether to throw one punch or a combination, and which punches in which order - and whether the defender can break the combination by stopping the middle punch.
5. **Head against body (what body work buys later).** What it is: head punches score and hurt now; body punches drain the other man's stamina, slow his feet, and buy head punches later. Lewis's round-2 body shot restricted Tua for 10 rounds. Trinidad's body work accumulated late. How often it matters: every bout, but it pays in later rounds (estimate - body work decides the championship rounds in 2 of 3 bouts). What the player decides: whether to work the head now or the body for later - and whether to spend stamina now to drain the other man's stamina.
6. **Defence kinds (block, parry, slip, roll, step back, clinch) and what each gives back.** What it is: block (stops the punch, gives nothing back), parry (turns the punch aside, may give a counter), slip (makes the punch miss, gives a counter), roll (makes the punch miss inside, gives a counter), step back (moves out of range, resets range, gives nothing back), clinch (stops the exchange, referee breaks, resets range, costs tiredness). How often it matters: every exchange (estimate - every punch is answered by one of these or lands clean). What the player decides: which defence to lay, and whether to take the safe defence that gives nothing back or the risky defence that gives a counter.
7. **Feints.** What it is: a punch that looks like a real punch but does nothing - it draws a defence, blinds, or measures. Trinidad feints his left and then throws the lead right. The jab as a feint is the same idea. How often it matters: every round (estimate - feints decide the lead in most rounds). What the player decides: whether to feint (pay stamina, draw a defence, set up the real punch) or to throw the real punch - and whether to bite on the feint (spend a defence) or to call it (let it through, risk the real punch).
8. **The ropes and the corner.** What it is: the ropes trap the man who is backing up; the corner traps the man who is cut off. Lewis ties up or spins out when Tua corners him. Joshua sags along the ropes when hurt. Trinidad pressures De La Hoya against the ropes. How often it matters: most bouts (estimate - the ropes or corner decide at least one exchange in most bouts). What the player decides: whether to back up (risk the ropes), to cut the ring (force the other man to the ropes), to tie up, or to spin out.
9. **Tiredness within a round and across rounds.** What it is: stamina spent throwing, taking, and holding; restored by the bell and the corner minute, but never fully. Joshua spends it all in round 5, has none in round 6, takes a round off, and gets a second wind. De La Hoya fades in round 9 and is weary in rounds 10-12. How often it matters: every bout after round 4 (estimate - tiredness decides the championship rounds in all 3 bouts). What the player decides: whether to spend stamina now or save it, whether to take a round off, and whether to work the body to drain the other man's stamina.
10. **Getting hurt, the knockdown, the count and surviving.** What it is: a big punch hurts; a bigger punch knocks down; the referee counts (10 seconds); the hurt man must get up, survive the round, and recover in the corner. Joshua is knocked down in round 6 and survives. Klitschko is knocked down in round 5, gets up quickly, and wins the next round. Klitschko is knocked down twice in round 11 and does not survive. How often it matters: in 1 of 3 bouts here (estimate - knockdowns decide about 1 in 3 bouts, but when they happen they decide the bout). What the player decides: whether to go for the finish (spend stamina, risk the counter) or to box (score, save stamina) - and whether the hurt man holds on, ties up, or takes the count.
11. **The bell.** What it is: 3 minutes of fighting, 1 minute of rest, repeated. The bell saves the hurt man, ends the round the leader is winning, and starts the round the man behind must win. Joshua survives round 6 because the bell comes. How often it matters: every round (estimate - the bell decides every round by ending it). What the player decides: whether to press for the finish before the bell or to save the finish for the next round - and whether to coast to the bell or to steal the round before it.
12. **The corner's minute.** What it is: 1 minute between rounds - water, ice, advice, a plan. Joshua's corner settles him after round 6 and tells him to take a round off. De La Hoya's corner treats the lead as safe. Tua's corner wets his hair down. How often it matters: every round (estimate - the corner minute decides the next round in most rounds). What the player decides: which plan to follow next round - work the body, counter-punch, box and move, go for the finish, or take a round off.
13. **The judges and stealing a round.** What it is: 10-point must scoring, round by round, on clean punching, effective aggression, ring generalship and defence. A knockdown is 10-8. A round won clearly is 10-9. The man who is still fighting in the championship rounds steals rounds the man who is coasting has already won on totals. De La Hoya lands 97 more punches and loses. How often it matters: every bout that goes to the cards (estimate - judges decide every decision bout). What the player decides: whether to coast on the lead (risk the judges) or to keep fighting (spend stamina, risk the counter) - and whether to steal a round the other man thinks he has won.
14. **A stoppage by the referee.** What it is: the referee stops the fight when the hurt man cannot defend himself, or when he is knocked down twice in one round, or when he is taking a barrage against the ropes. Joshua-Klitschko round 11. How often it matters: in 1 of 3 bouts here (estimate - stoppages decide about 1 in 3 bouts). What the player decides: whether to go for the stoppage (spend stamina, throw the barrage) or to box - and whether the hurt man's corner throws the towel or the hurt man holds on.

## Part 3 - Card against card

Two different ways to turn Part 2 into a turn of cards. Both use the references in our own words. Both have no dice. Both show what will happen before a card is played (once both cards are laid, the outcome is shown and cannot be changed). Both are for boxer v boxer only.

Common to both ways (so the walks can be compared): 12 rounds (10 rounds for a non-title bout - UNSURE, second seat to confirm the card game should use 12 or 10; the walks use 12 because all three bouts are 12-round title bouts). Each man has his own deck (not a shared deck - ours is two boxers with their own styles, unlike the shared 52 in one reference game). Hand of 5, draw to 5 at the start of each round, keep unplayed cards between rounds (so a held card stays held). Each man has a Head track (damage taken; a big punch on a hurt man knocks down), a Body/Stamina track (body work drains it; tiredness; a clean body punch on a man with no stamina left is a knockdown - the body stoppage without dice, from Mixed Martial Cards), a Range track (long range / punching range / clinch / ropes - the man who sets the range decides which cards are legal), and a Round score (judges, 10-point must, scored at the bell). The bout ends by knockout (Head to zero), body knockout (Body/Stamina to zero), referee stoppage (two knockdowns in one round, or a barrage on a hurt man - the referee's call, shown before the cards resolve), or the clock (judges' cards). Decisions a bout: 22 to 45 for one player (the owner's bar). Both ways below aim for 36 decisions (12 rounds x 3 exchanges/clashes).

### Way 1 - The Lead (from Mixed Martial Cards, simplified to one answer window)

**The turn.** One man has the Lead (the initiative). One exchange is one decision. In an exchange:

1. The man with the Lead lays one attack card face up, and chooses a hidden mode: Work (normal), Full (full power - costs more stamina, does more, leaves him open), or Feint (looks like the attack, does nothing - if the defender spends a defence on it, the next attack is free; if the defender calls it and lets it through, the attacker loses the Lead). The mode is hidden. The card is shown.
2. The defender, seeing the card but not the mode, lays one answer card (block, parry, slip, roll, step back, clinch, or a counter - or none, pass and take it). He may also pre-commit a prediction answer before seeing the card (some dodges and counters only work as a prediction) - simplified here to one answer window after seeing the card, because three answer windows a turn is too long for us (Mixed Martial Cards has prediction, reaction and surprise windows; we keep only the reaction window and note what we cut).
3. The exchange resolves in order, with the outcome shown before it resolves. If the answer beats the attack, the attack fails and the Lead passes to the defender (he attacks next exchange). If the answer fails or there is no answer, the attack lands and the Lead stays with the attacker (he attacks again). A combination card keeps the Lead even when blocked, unless the attacker takes counter damage. A landed counter takes the Lead back at once.
4. A long exchange (holding the Lead for consecutive exchanges) makes every further attack cost more stamina (Mixed Martial Cards: a long exchange makes every further attack cost more). The attacker may stop the exchange early (end his turn) - he cannot be forced to keep attacking.

A round is 3 exchanges. 12 rounds x 3 = 36 decisions. The bell ends the round. The corner minute is a choice of plan, not a heal: at the bell the player picks one plan card of three he brought, face up for the next round, with a once-a-round power (for example "work the body" - body punches drain more this round; "counter-punch" - your counter does more once; "box and move" - one Slip costs less). Water and ice stay as the fallback. A knockdown knocks the plan card off the table (from Kungfu Card's stance rule - a knockdown knocks the stance off). The plan lies face up, so he plays round it.

**Hidden and shown.** Shown: the attack card, the answer card, the Lead, the range, the stamina tracks, the round score so far. Hidden: the mode (Work/Full/Feint), the hands. The feint is the hidden bit: the defender sees the card but not the mode. If he spends a defence on a feint he is thrown off for the next attack; if he calls it the attacker loses the Lead; if he tries to counter a feint he is left wide open. The table counts how many feints he has thrown this bout (the feint with a warning, from our earlier notes). A 20-second clock on the exchange is optional (Mixed Martial Cards and MMA Arena both use 20 seconds; the owner's bar is fast and punchy).

**Scoring.** Judges score each round 10-point must on clean punches, ring generalship (who held the Lead, who controlled range), effective aggression and defence, and knockdowns (10-8). Judge points are earned whenever you do damage (Mixed Martial Cards). The bout goes to the cards unless there is a knockout, body knockout or stoppage. The leader on points cannot coast for free because ring generalship is scored and tiredness returns with rest (see feedback loop below).

**Way 1 - the eight questions, one line each.**

1. Why would a bout go to the judges? Because Head and Body/Stamina are separate from the round score, and judge points are earned for damage and ring generalship each round, so a bout that goes 12 rounds with no knockout is scored on the cards.
2. Why is going first or second not an advantage? Because a defended attack hands the Lead over, so the man who goes second must defend to get the Lead, and holding the Lead costs more stamina each consecutive exchange.
3. Why would a player throw a combination, a body punch, a feint? A combination keeps the Lead even when blocked; a body punch drains the other man's stamina and buys head punches later; a feint draws a defence and makes the next attack free - and the other man answers each with a block, a slip, a clinch, or by calling the feint and taking the Lead.
4. What is hidden and what is shown? Shown: the attack card, the answer card, the Lead, the range, the stamina tracks, the round score; hidden: the mode (Work/Full/Feint) and the hands.
5. How long is a bout in decisions? 36 decisions (12 rounds x 3 exchanges), inside the 22 to 45 bar.
6. What does a weak player do, and how does a skilled one beat him? The weak player throws Work attacks until his stamina is gone and bites on every feint; the skilled one counts the other man's stamina, feints when the other man has a counter ready, and ends the exchange before it gets dear.
7. What does the corner minute decide? Which plan card to lay face up for the next round (work the body, counter-punch, box and move, take a round off), with a once-a-round power, and a knockdown knocks the plan off the table.
8. Which numbers would a later probe measure to say it works? Goes to the cards in 40 to 70% of bot bouts; going first wins 45 to 55%; every card laid in 2%+ of the bouts where it was legal; a combination in most bouts; 22 to 45 decisions; empty exchanges under 10%.

**Way 1 - feedback loop and cap.** Loop: the leader on points stops throwing (De La Hoya rounds 10-12) - he holds the Lead, throws Feints, and lets the judges decide. Cap: ring generalship is scored (holding the Lead without throwing clean punches loses the round), tiredness returns with rest (the bell restores stamina, so coasting does not save stamina for later), and a Feint that is called costs the Lead. Second loop: a hurt man gets hurt more (knockdown, then more knockdowns). Cap: the count, the bell, and the three-knockdown stop (two knockdowns in one round is a stoppage).

### Way 2 - The Clash (from Kungfu Card, with the held-card rule from Clutchtime)

**The turn.** A round is up to three clashes. One clash is one decision. In a clash:

1. The attacker lays one card face up. The defender, seeing the attacker's card, lays one card face up. Both cards are shown. The outcome is shown before it resolves and cannot be changed.
2. The clash resolves card against card, in order, with no dice: move against move, the lower number takes the difference (the man who laid the lower card takes the difference as damage); a technique against a move does its full damage; a defence stops a move outright and half of a technique (rounded up). In boxing words: Jab against Block is blocked; Slip against Jab is slipped and the slipper counters; Body Hook against Block goes under (the block is for the head); Uppercut against Block goes through (the uppercut is unblockable - it is a technique, not a move); Counter against a lead does its full damage (the counter is a technique).
3. The man who took more damage in the last round attacks first in the next round (the comeback rule, from Kungfu Card). The beaten side gets the lead. If the attacker and defender took equal damage, the same attacker attacks again (UNSURE - the reference rule as read says otherwise the same attacker again; second seat to confirm).
4. After at least one clash the attacker may stop the round early (end the round). The defender then takes back one of his cards after resolution. Not allowed two rounds running (from Kungfu Card - the stop-early rule).
5. The disrupt, the heart of it: a move card that completes three of a kind or a run of three, counting the cards both players have laid in the round, cuts the round off - the rest is skipped, the disrupter draws one of his technique cards, flips the top move of the deck for that much damage, and knocks the other's stance card (here: the corner plan card) off the table. In boxing words: Jab, Cross, Hook landed in that order in one round (across both men's laid cards) is a combination - it draws a finisher card (a big single punch you cannot put in a deck). The defender breaks it by stopping the middle card, so the one defence now has a second reason beside damage. (From Kungfu Card's disrupt; from our earlier notes: a pattern across both boxers' cards.)
6. A held dear card gets 1 cheaper every clash it stays in the hand (from Clutchtime's held card - one big shot gets 1 cheaper every turn it stays in the hand). The table shows how long you have held it, so it is a threat that grows in the open. In boxing words: the Uppercut at full price is bad against everybody; held for two clashes it is cheap enough to throw.
7. A clean body punch on a boxer with no stamina left is a knockdown (the body stoppage without dice, from Mixed Martial Cards - stamina overkill gives a chance of a body knockout; our no-dice version is a knockdown). It gives the body deck a way to finish and makes "keep one stamina" a real decision.

A round is 3 clashes (or fewer if the attacker stops it early or a disrupt cuts it off). 12 rounds x 3 = 36 decisions. The bell ends the round. The corner minute is a plan card (as in Way 1, from Mixed Martial Cards and Kungfu Card stances - one plan of three, face up, once-a-round power, knocked off by a knockdown or a disrupt).

**Hidden and shown.** Shown: every card laid, both plans, the deck's make-up (both players can count what is left), the held-card count (how long a dear card has been held). Hidden: the hands. No mode is hidden (unlike Way 1) - the feint is a card, not a mode: the Feint card looks like a Jab (it is laid as a Jab, and the defender must answer it as a Jab), but it does nothing; if the defender spends a defence on it, the attacker's next card in the round is free; if the defender lets it through, nothing happens and the attacker paid stamina. The table counts how many Feints he has thrown this bout (the feint with a warning, from our earlier notes).

**Scoring.** Judges score each round 10-point must on the clashes (who won each clash), knockdowns (10-8), ring generalship (who controlled range, who held the lead), effective aggression and defence. The bout goes to the cards unless there is a knockout, body knockout or stoppage. The leader on points cannot coast for free because the attacker may stop the round early (coasting is a decision with a cost - the defender takes back a card) and ring generalship is scored (see feedback loop below).

That fix is a rule, not a tuned value, and it is in Part 4.

**Way 2 - the eight questions, one line each.**

1. Why would a bout go to the judges? Because damage per clash is separate from the round score, and judges score each round 10-point must on clash winners, knockdowns, ring generalship and defence, so a bout that goes 12 rounds with no knockout is scored on the cards.
2. Why is going first or second not an advantage? Because the man who took more damage last round attacks first next round (the comeback rule), so the defender who wins the clash by taking less damage does not get the lead - the man who took more does.
3. Why would a player throw a combination, a body punch, a feint? A combination (three of a kind or a run of three across both men's laid cards) cuts the round off and draws a finisher; a body punch drains stamina and a clean body punch on a man with no stamina is a knockdown; a feint draws a defence and makes the next card free - and the other man answers each by stopping the middle card of the combination, by blocking the body punch, or by calling the feint.
4. What is hidden and what is shown? Shown: every card laid, both plans, the deck's make-up, the held-card count; hidden: the hands.
5. How long is a bout in decisions? 36 decisions (12 rounds x 3 clashes, fewer if the attacker stops a round early or a disrupt cuts it off), inside the 22 to 45 bar.
6. What does a weak player do, and how does a skilled one beat him? The weak player plays his biggest card each clash and never holds the dear card or stops a round early; the skilled one holds the dear card until it is cheap, feeds a low card to finish his own run, and stops the round one clash early when ahead.
7. What does the corner minute decide? Which plan card to lay face up for the next round (work the body, counter-punch, box and move, take a round off), with a once-a-round power, and a knockdown or a disrupt knocks the plan off the table.
8. Which numbers would a later probe measure to say it works? Goes to the cards in 40 to 70% of bot bouts; going first wins 45 to 55%; every card laid in 2%+ of the bouts where it was legal; a combination (disrupt) in most bouts; 22 to 45 decisions; empty clashes under 10%.

**Way 2 - feedback loop and cap.** Loop: the leader on points stops throwing (De La Hoya rounds 10-12) - he stops rounds early every round and lets the judges decide. Cap: stopping a round early is not allowed two rounds running (from Kungfu Card), ring generalship is scored (stopping early without winning the clashes loses the round), and the defender takes back a card when the round is stopped early (coasting gives the other man a card back). Second loop: a hurt man gets hurt more (knockdown, then more knockdowns). Cap: the count, the bell, and the three-knockdown stop (two knockdowns in one round is a stoppage).

## Part 4 - Your pick

**Pick: Way 2, The Clash (Kungfu Card, with the held-card rule from Clutchtime). Build it first.**

Two lines: Way 2 has no hidden mode, so the game shows what will happen before a card is played and the owner can see every number; the combination fires as a pattern across both men's cards, the comeback rule fixes the second-player advantage, and 36 decisions fits the fast and punchy bar. Way 1's hidden mode is a guess, which the owner has already measured as a guess or a lookup, and Mixed Martial Cards is too long for us (20 minutes a match, three answer windows a turn) - Way 1 simplified to one window still needs the hidden mode to work, and the hidden mode is the part the owner dislikes.

**The boxing deck (14 cards, name and what it does, in words).** No tuned values. Each card is in our own words. The deck is 14 cards (10 to 15 as required). Each man has his own deck (the same 14 cards, or a style deck built from these 14 - UNSURE, second seat to confirm whether the card game should use one shared boxing deck or two style decks; the walk assumes one shared boxing deck for boxer v boxer).

1. **Jab.** Scores, blinds, measures the distance, and sets up the straight right and the hook. The jab is the range. Throw it to score, to blind, to measure, or as a feint (see Feint). The defender answers it with a block, a slip, a roll, a step back, a clinch, or by taking it.
2. **Straight Right (Cross).** The power punch behind the jab. Scores big, counters a lead, and knocks down a hurt man. The defender answers it with a block, a slip, a roll, a clinch, or by taking it. A tired man's straight right is slower (see tiredness rule below).
3. **Lead Hook.** Scores, works inside, and counters. The defender answers it with a block, a slip, a roll, a clinch, or by taking it.
4. **Body Hook.** Works the body. Drains the other man's stamina, slows his feet, and buys head punches later. A clean body punch on a man with no stamina left is a knockdown (the body stoppage without dice). The defender answers it by blocking the body, stepping back, clinching, or by taking it. The body hook is the reason to throw a body punch: drain now, knock down later.
5. **Uppercut.** The dear card. Big damage, goes through the block (it is a technique, not a move). Held in the hand, it gets cheaper every clash it stays held (from Clutchtime). The defender answers it with a slip, a roll, a step back, a clinch, or by taking it - the block does not stop it. The uppercut is the reason to hold a card: wait until it is cheap, then throw it where the block is waiting.
6. **One-Two (Jab-Cross).** A combination of two punches. The first is seen, the second lands because the defender has used his defence on the first. The defender breaks it by stopping the first punch. The one-two is the reason to throw a combination: the second punch lands when the first is seen.
7. **Jab-Cross-Hook.** A combination of three punches in order. Completes a run of three across both men's laid cards and cuts the round off (the disrupt, from Kungfu Card) - the disrupter draws a finisher card. The defender breaks it by stopping the middle card (the Cross). The jab-cross-hook is the reason to build a pattern: lay the Jab, then the Cross, then the Hook, and the round is yours.
8. **Slip.** Makes the punch miss and gives a counter. The defender lays it against a lead punch (Jab, Straight Right, Lead Hook). A tired man's slip costs more (see tiredness rule below) - when tired, the slip is dear or fails. The slip is the reason to defend: miss the punch, throw the counter.
9. **Roll.** Makes the punch miss inside and gives a counter. Works at punching range and inside. The defender lays it against a power punch. A tired man's roll costs more. The roll is the reason to defend inside: miss the hook, throw the counter.
10. **Block/Parry.** Stops the punch and gives nothing back. Safe, but loses the round if you do it every clash (ring generalship is scored). The defender lays it against a move (Jab, Straight Right, Lead Hook, One-Two). It does not stop a technique (Uppercut, Counter). The block is the reason to defend safely: stop the punch, take no damage, lose the round on points if you do nothing else.
11. **Step Back.** Moves out of range and resets range. Gives nothing back. The defender lays it against a lead punch to reset to long range. The step back is the reason to defend by moving: out of range, back to the jab.
12. **Clinch (Tie Up).** Stops the exchange, the referee breaks, range resets to punching range. Costs tiredness (holding is work). The defender lays it against any punch to stop the exchange. The clinch is the reason to defend when hurt or tired: stop the exchange, referee breaks, bell comes closer.
13. **Feint.** Looks like a Jab (it is laid as a Jab, and the defender must answer it as a Jab), but does nothing. If the defender spends a defence on it, the attacker's next card in the round is free. If the defender lets it through, nothing happens and the attacker paid stamina. The table counts how many Feints he has thrown this bout (the feint with a warning). The feint is the reason to throw a punch that does nothing: draw a defence, then throw the real punch free.
14. **Counter (Check Hook / Straight Counter).** A technique that beats a lead and does its full damage. It only works as an answer to a lead punch (Jab, Straight Right, Lead Hook). Laid against a defence or a feint, it does nothing and leaves the counter-puncher open. The counter is the reason to wait: let him lead, then counter.

**The tiredness rule (the fix both walks missed).** A tired man's defence costs more or fails. In words: when a man's stamina is low, his Slip, Roll and Block cost more stamina to lay, and a Full-power punch (Straight Right, Uppercut) beats a tired man's Slip (the slip is late). This is not a tuned value - it is a bar: a tired man must be easier to hit than a fresh man, or the knockdown never happens. The probe will measure whether knockdowns happen in the championship rounds (see bars below).

**Three things most likely to go wrong when bots play it.**

1. **Bots always lay the biggest card and never hold the dear card, never stop a round early, and never build a pattern.** The bot will play the Uppercut at full price in Clash 1, never hold it until it is cheap, never stop a round early when ahead, and never lay Jab, Cross, Hook in order to complete the run. The combination (disrupt) will fire 0 times a bout, the held-card rule will never matter, and the stop-early decision will never be made. Cap: the probe measures every card laid in 2%+ of the bouts where it was legal, a combination in most bouts, and empty clashes under 10% - if the bot fails these, the bot is wrong, not the cards. The bot must be taught to hold, to stop, and to build.
2. **Bots ignore the body and never feint.** The bot will work the head every clash, never throw the Body Hook, never drain stamina, and never throw the Feint, because the head scores now and the body pays later. The body stoppage will never happen, the Feint will never be laid, and the game will be head-only trading. Cap: the probe measures the Body Hook laid in 2%+ of the bouts where it was legal and the Feint laid in 2%+ of the bouts where it was legal - if the bot fails these, the bot is wrong. The bot must be taught that body work buys the knockdown and the feint buys the free card.
3. **Bots coast when ahead on points or go for the finish when hurt.** The leader on points will stop rounds early every round and let the judges decide (De La Hoya rounds 10-12), or the hurt man will throw the Uppercut at full price and get countered. The leader will win on points without fighting, or the hurt man will be knocked down twice in one round. Cap: stopping a round early is not allowed two rounds running, ring generalship is scored (stopping early without winning the clashes loses the round), the defender takes back a card when the round is stopped early, the count and the bell save the hurt man, and two knockdowns in one round is a stoppage. The probe measures goes to the cards in 40 to 70% of bot bouts (not 100%, not 0%) and going first wins 45 to 55% - if the leader always wins on points or the hurt man always gets hurt more, the caps are wrong.

## Appendix - What goes, what stays, and the bars

**What goes from the current game (boxer v boxer).** Sprawl and Escape (3 of 15 cards, never used boxer v boxer, not boxing). The ground, the guard, the hold, the sweep, the pass (not boxing). The race to zero (both men end near zero health, every bout ends by knockout) - replaced by Head, Body/Stamina, Range and Round score as separate tracks, with the bout going to the cards unless there is a knockout, body knockout or stoppage. The combination rule that fires 0.02 times a bout (Jab, Cross, Hook clean in order in one volley) - replaced by the disrupt pattern across both men's laid cards (three of a kind or a run of three in one round), which fires in most bouts.

**What stays (rebuilt).** Jab, Cross (Straight Right), Lead Hook, Body Hook, Uppercut, Slip, Block - all in the deck above, rebuilt as clash cards with the tiredness rule. The wind pool (stamina) - rebuilt as Body/Stamina with body drain and the body stoppage. The corner minute - rebuilt as a plan card, not a heal. The judges - rebuilt as 10-point must round scoring, not a single points total.

**The bars (not tuned values).** A later probe measures, over 400 bot bouts: goes to the cards in 40 to 70% of bot bouts; going first wins 45 to 55%; every card of the deck laid in 2%+ of the bouts where it was legal; a combination (disrupt) in most bouts; 22 to 45 decisions a bout for one player; empty clashes under 10%; knockdowns in the championship rounds (tiredness matters); body stoppages in some bouts (body work matters); feints laid in some bouts (feints matter). If the probe fails these bars, the cards or the bot are wrong. The bars are not tuned values - they are the pass/fail lines.

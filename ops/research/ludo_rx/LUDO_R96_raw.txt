Lead Game Designer Playtest & Floor Evaluation: Round 96 (S234)
1. Gym Floor Rating (1–10)
(a) Fun minute-to-minute: 4/10
Reason: You configure your boxer’s next block in ten seconds, then sit back passively watching the clock tick while floating numbers drift upward like a low-interaction idle screensaver.
(b) Clarity (do I know what to do and why): 7/10
Reason: The station pick UI with explicit BEST/GOOD tags and the floating gain bubbles explain immediate cause-and-effect well, though the long-term trade-offs between station stats and technique prerequisites remain obscured.
(c) Meaningful decisions: 3/10
Reason: The game systematically solves itself: the BEST recommendation tells you where to click, the active cue stays fixed on whichever matches your stamina threshold, and true managerial dilemmas are nonexistent.
(d) Feedback / juice: 6/10
Reason: The S234 custom workout animations and Game Dev Tycoon-style gain bubbles give respectable tactile polish, but they do not communicate physical exertion, technical friction, or tactile impact.
(e) Does it make me want one more day: 4/10
Reason: The compulsion loop lives on the calendar (booking fights, watching purse payouts, fight night), not on the gym floor; surviving the gym floor feels like an administrative chore you endure to reach the bout.
2. Is It a Boring Act Today?

YES.

Why and Where Attention Drops

The floor currently plays like a monitoring chore rather than a managerial simulation.

Attention collapses completely the moment the boxer begins a station block.

The Setup (0:00–0:10): The player selects the fighter, picks the station marked BEST, sets a cue (e.g., CRISP FORM if drilling a move card, or PACE YOURSELF if stamina is dipping), and queues the next station.
The Dead Zone (0:10–end of block): For the remaining 90–120 in-game minutes, there is zero meaningful input. The boxer walks, runs their station loop animation, bubbles float up, and the clock ticks down.
The Autopilot Trap: Because the station pick explicitly surfaces optimal tags (BEST / GOOD), player agency is bypassed. The player isn’t diagnosing boxer deficiencies against an upcoming opponent's profile; they are merely confirming the UI's calculation. Furthermore, the 3 top cues (POWER THROUGH, CRISP FORM, PACE YOURSELF) act as static toggles rather than dynamic interventions.
3. Mechanics Missing (Ranked by Fun Gained / Build Cost)

To elevate FightCamp into a true PC management sim in the vein of Football Manager, Punch Club, and Motorsport Manager, the gym floor must transition from a passive progress bar to an arena of tension, trade-offs, and micro-crises.

1. Form Quality / Fatigue Breakdown (Friction & Micro-Intervention)
What the player does: As stamina depletes or localized body-region wear (left_arm, torso_core, etc.) climbs past threshold limits, the boxer’s form degrades visually and mechanically. The player must intervene: call out an audible correction ("Check your base!"), ease the tempo to salvage form, or pull them off the station early to prevent sloppy habit formation.
What it changes in the numbers: When form breaks down, gains decay into "Bad Reps" (diminishing stat gains, -15% technique efficiency, +35% wear to that body region, risk of soft-tissue sprain). Catching it early preserves technique gains and builds ring_iq.
Frequency: 2 to 4 times per in-game day (typically at the tail-end of heavy training blocks).
Reference: Motorsport Manager (managing tire degradation cliffs and driving style modes mid-stint).
2. Sparring Spar-Partner Dynamics & Flash Moments
What the player does: When the ring is used, it’s not an isolated stat mill. The player hires/selects specific gym members or paid journeymen with known styles (e.g., southpaw slugger, outside rangy mover). Mid-round, an event flash pops: your boxer drops his rear hand on the exit; you choose on the fly: Let him eat the counter to learn the hard way (+ring_iq, -chin/morale) or Halt the round and drill the reset (+defense, interrupts tempo).
What it changes in the numbers: Rapidly tests and builds branch techniques (e.g., boxing_slip_counter or boxing_pull_counter), shifts boxer morale and composure, and applies targeted wear to head_neck.
Frequency: 1 time per day on scheduled sparring days (2–3 times per week).
Reference: Football Manager (training ground match preparation and tactical match-engine incident prompts).
3. Coach Direct Instruction vs. Hands-Off Split (Coach Capacity / Bandwidth)
What the player does: The Head Coach cannot be everywhere. Stations operate at base baseline efficiency unless assigned a coach. The player must assign the coach’s active attention: holding pads for your prospect, supervising the rack, or giving advice to gym walk-ins to keep gym income high.
What it changes in the numbers: Unsupervised stations produce 60% standard gains and flat ring_iq. Coach-supervised stations grant +25% stat acceleration, unlock technique node mastery progress, and mitigate body wear by 30%.
Frequency: 1 to 3 allocation decisions per day depending on the training schedule.
Reference: Two Point Hospital / RimWorld (staff allocation, zone priority, and specialized labor management).
4. Camp Milestones & Opposition Game-Planning Context
What the player does: Give the floor context from the booked fight. Pin an opponent report on the clipboard (e.g., "Opponent is an aggressive inside puncher with devastating hooks"). The floor UI visually highlights which stations and techniques directly counter the threat (e.g., drilling boxing_long_guard, boxing_clinch_tieup, or building footwork via agility ladder).
What it changes in the numbers: Satisfying camp preparation targets grants "Gameplan Readiness" (0–100%). High readiness translates directly into automated execution bonuses in the fight engine (faster reaction time against hooks, tighter defensive posture).
Frequency: Static context updated every morning; evaluated at day wrap.
Reference: Punch Club (stat target optimization for upcoming fight profiles) and Football Manager (opposition reports driving weekly training schedules).
4. Cut List (Noise Without Decisions)
The Walk-in Street Greeter Interruption:
Problem: Having random members of the public walk into reception demanding immediate manual clicks ("Greet", "Trial Session", "Come back later") while you are monitoring camp rhythm is casual busywork. It is pure click friction that distracts from training.
Fix: Automate reception via gym policy settings (e.g., "Auto-accept paying trials", "Refer to assistant coach") or move walk-in resolution to the morning office batch.
Explicit "BEST" / "GOOD" Tags on Station Selection:
Problem: It removes the "manager" from the manager sim. When the UI explicitly tells the player the mathematically optimal station, the game becomes a mindless click-the-highlight exercise.
Fix: Remove the explicit grading text. Instead, display the boxer's stat deltas, deficient prerequisites for their target move card (e.g., "Needs 28 Speed for boxing_circle_lead; current Speed: 26"), and let the player deduce that the speed bag or agility ladder is required.
Redundant Station Upgrades on the Floor:
Problem: Having "Upgrade Station" as an inline context button inside the real-time floor loop mixes long-term facility capital investment with daily workout scheduling.
Fix: Restrict facility improvements and capital expenditures entirely to the Gym Office / Facilities screen.
5. The ONE Change to Build First
The "Form Breakdown & Audible" System (Fatigue Quality Threshold)

Why this first:

It attacks the primary structural flaw of the game: the zero-input dead zone while the station workout is running.

Right now, you press go, and you walk away mentally. With Form Breakdown:

As stamina drops into the red or wear on a specific body region (like left_arm during repeated jab drills) spikes, a visual fatigue marker and audio cue fire (boxer breathes heavy, mechanics look sluggish, gain bubbles turn yellow/red to show dirty reps).
The player has two immediate hotkey choices: "Correct Form" (spends mental focus/coach bandwidth to steady the boxer for clean reps) or "Call the Rep" (cuts the set short, preserving recovery for the next station).

This transforms the clock-tick phase from dead downtime into active, critical observation—the exact sensation of a coach leaning against the ropes watching a fighter's hips and hands in real time. It costs very little engine work (leverages existing Godot signals, stamina counters, and floating bubble pipelines), but fundamentally fixes the core loop.

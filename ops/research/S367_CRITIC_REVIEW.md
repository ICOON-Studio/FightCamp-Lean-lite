# FightCamp - early access build (update 66) - a critic's preview (S367, 2026-11-02)

How it was played: the real game driven by a script on the hidden desktop (layout_snapshot.gd: a steady player's first
seven weeks and a first fight, 54 screens) plus the BJJ side (s363_stills.gd, 6 screens), on branch s365-bjj-side.
Not hand-played, no audio. The 20-career balance harness was not run (the PC was AMBER while a film rendered).

## Verdict: 6.5 / 10 as a preview (was 7 before the fight film was judged)
A smart, honest boxing-management sim whose decisions matter and - rarer - explain themselves afterwards, wrapped in
management screens that still look like an admin panel and a fight that does not yet sell the drama its numbers describe.
Systems 8 - Clarity 6 - Presentation 5 (the whiteboard 8, the rest 4) - The fight 3 (Gemini 4 Argon on 30 s of a live
round: 2/10 - clinches merge, the referee's break passes through a boxer, hits react without contact, ~60-70% speed;
the two worst moments checked on full-resolution frames).

## The first hour
The title promises "Run a boxing gym. Build a fighter. Win on Saturday." and the game keeps that promise quickly. Quick
start opens on a hand-drawn whiteboard with two calls to action: a walk-in at reception and a sponsor. The walk-in is the
best screen in the game: Lucy Jackson, 21, out-boxer, 3-1, "I've had four fights at a church hall, I want a proper gym
and a proper coach." Her card gives traits (big heart, short fuse), a habit (chases the stoppage), a signature jab, an
ambition, attributes against their ceilings - and the first real decision: sharpen the signature, fix the habit or raise
the ceiling. Within a minute I cared about her.

## The loop
Week by week: train, read the review, answer offers, set a camp, make weight, fight. The offers screen colour-codes
risk ("Not safe: she has 4 bouts, Whitfield has 16"); the camp is a clean three-way trade (focus, protocol Light / $300
Standard / $700 Hard, $150 film study). Fight day lands well: the weight line turns green with a tick, a halftone poster
pins itself to the board.

The weak beat is the week itself. The week review is a ledger: hand speed +0.09, ring IQ +0.2, and a "planned vs actual"
table of grey-on-grey "on plan" rows. Decimal gains make a week feel like nothing happened. The BJJ side already shows the
fix - its week review ends on a sticky note, "NEW WEAPON: Ana's armbar from guard." Boxing needs that moment every week.

## Fight night
The corner minute is excellent: who won the round and why ("Maria took it on the work done in them"), exchanges,
punches, body shots and energy side by side, a coach's read ("Get inside and go to the body: it slows Maria down for
later") and four decisions (fix a habit, recover, set-up, talk). The result screen is the game's signature: a split
decision (29-28, 29-28, 28-29) and WHAT DECIDED IT - peak camp, film study, conditioning, and "your corner call before
round 2 won that round". Few management games close the loop between your choices and the outcome this clearly.

The 3D fight itself is the weakest link. The opening shot looks through the ropes at one boxer in a dark arena with a
low-detail crowd (the live camera, from the film, is inside the ropes and frames both boxers and the referee); the drama
lives in the corner and result screens, not in the ring. A Gemini pass on three in-game clips
flagged the rear hook dropping both hands to the hips before it lands and the hurt knockback reading as a duck.

## Depth
Real and layered: the Playbook (12 of 27 moves ready, grouped by range, each READY / USABLE / RAW, with a 3D ghost
partner to watch it), the fighter page (radar, ceilings, mind stats, a yellow "at a glance" note), goals with deadlines,
the nationals ladder, money with a runway. The no-gi side is a second game: a belt road from white to black, a syllabus
of owned moves, the stripe as the coach's call with costs, opens each decided on one group, and the mat as a business
(8 members at $110 a month, a hired coach or Ana teaching).

## Presentation and clarity
The whiteboard home, polaroid, poster and sticky notes are a coherent, likeable identity. Most other screens are a list
in the top fifth of an empty page (Fighters > Training, Fights > Offers, Money): functional, airless. Density spikes the
other way in places - the perk road reads "def 65 (57) + check hook 30 (0)", monospace lines sit inside sans-serif cards.
The dashed-red glossary words help. Low-contrast text (planned vs actual, the result's body report) is hard to read.

## Small things a reviewer notices
- The title screen said "Week 3 build" at update 66 (fixed in S367: it no longer names a build).
- A sponsor offer sat on the home screen for five weeks with no deadline or consequence; offers should expire or press.
- Mail climbs to 22 unread in seven weeks: noise, or the player is not being told what matters.

## What would make it great
1. End every week on a reveal (a new weapon, a member milestone, a rival's result) - a moment, not a ledger.
2. Give the management screens a layout: two columns, cards, the fighter's face - no half-empty pages.
3. Sell the fight: lens inside the ropes, both boxers framed, a brighter arena; replace the flagged clips (Gemini first).
4. Offers that expire or escalate; mail that says what needs you.
5. Show progress as whole points or "x to the next point", not decimals.

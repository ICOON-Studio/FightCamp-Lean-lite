I'll work out five defence ideas now.

All five use one shared rule, so that every result shows before the attacker commits. The defender decides early, at the end of the defender's own lead. Defence sits face up and goes off by itself, on the first punch it can stop. The attacker builds the combination looking at it, and the preview shows exactly what will land. The damage numbers are only examples: jab 1, cross 3, lead hook 3.

**1. EARNED GUARD CARDS** (cards created by what you do)
- **The rule.** What you do in your own lead creates defence cards in a small row, three at most, lasting until your next lead ends:
  - a hand that throws nothing in your lead creates **Glove Home** on that side, which blocks one head punch from that side;
  - a jab that lands creates **Range**, which makes the last punch of the next combination fall short;
  - a feint the other boxer reacts to (the feint's target was covered) creates **Slip**, which dodges one straight to the head and adds a free jab counter to your next lead;
  - throwing two moves or fewer creates **Set**, which takes half off one body punch.
  
  Uppercuts go through Glove Home, as the cards already say.
- **One exchange.** In the defender's last lead it was lead jab, then lead jab. The jab landed and the rear hand never threw, so the row holds Glove Home (rear side) and Range. Now the attacker builds jab, cross, lead hook and sees the preview:
  - the jab lands for 1;
  - Glove Home sits on the rear side, so it covers punches from the side of the attacker's lead hand, and the cross arrives on the other side and lands for 3;
  - Range makes the last punch, the lead hook, fall short.
  
  The defender takes 4 instead of 7. The attacker could instead swap the lead hook for a rear uppercut, which goes through Glove Home and isn't the last punch blocked by Range. The preview shows that choice too.
- **The decision.** Every lead becomes a trade between damage now and safety in the next lead. A short combination built on one hand earns Glove Home and Set but does little. A three-move combination that switches hands does the most damage but leaves the row empty. Go defensive when you're low on Health or Stamina. Go full when the other boxer has no row worth breaking through.
- **How it can break.** Jab, jab, stop every lead keeps Range and Glove Home up forever, and wins on points. **The cap:** Range comes only from a jab that lands, and a jab into a held Glove Home doesn't land. Two leads in a row of jabs only also hand the other boxer a Slip.
- **The real boxing.** The hand that doesn't punch stays home, and the jab keeps the other boxer out at the end of the punch.

**2. HOW YOU FINISH IS HOW YOU STAND**
- **The rule.** Every move card has a "leaves you" line printed on it. Lead moves leave you **guard high**. Body punches leave you **low** (head open, ribs covered). Rear power moves leave you **weight forward** (open, but your next lead's first punch is stronger). The last move of your combination sets your defence for the whole of the other boxer's lead. There are no extra cards.
- **One exchange.** The defender finished on a rear overhand, so the defender is weight forward. The attacker's jab, cross and lead hook all land, for 7. Had the defender finished on a jab instead (guard high), the jab and cross would have been blocked down to 1 each and only the hook would have landed: 5. Had the defender finished low, the hook would have landed clean.
- **The decision.** "Which punch do I end on?" becomes the real call. A big finish now means taking more back. Finish safe when the other boxer has power cards face up. Finish heavy when that boxer is tired or holds only jabs.
- **How it can break.** Always ending on a jab means never getting hit hard. **The cap:** end the same way twice in a row and the other boxer gets **Read**: that boxer's first punch ignores your guard. A jab finish also never gets the power bonus.
- **The real boxing.** "Never finish on the right hand and stand there": the combination ends with defence, or you pay for it.

**3. THE RING: GIVING GROUND**
- **The rule.** A five-square track runs from the centre to the ropes. Before the other boxer's lead, the defender can buy up to two **Steps Back**, each for one square of ring. Each step makes one punch of the defender's choice miss, and the choice is shown in the attacker's preview. On the ropes there are no steps, only **Cover**, which halves one punch. Each lead you finish with three moves wins back one square, because you walked your opponent back.
- **One exchange.** The defender stands two squares from the ropes and buys one step. The preview shows the step spent on the cross, so the jab lands for 1, the cross misses, and the hook lands for 3. That's 4 taken, with one square left. The attacker could add a step forward (a feint that cuts the ring) so that the step costs two squares.
- **The decision.** Ring space is a second health bar. Spend it early to stay fresh and you get trapped late. Hold it and you take damage now. Step back from the power punch, not the jab. Stop when one square is left, because the ropes cost more than any one punch.
- **How it can break.** Two steps every lead in round 1, then winning the space back. **The cap:** a defender who steps back in two leads in a row gives the attacker **Cut Off**, so the attacker's next lead starts one square closer. Only throwing three moves wins space back, and that costs Stamina.
- **The real boxing.** Ring generalship: footwork saves your chin and costs you the ropes.

**4. TWO-FACED CARDS: THE HAND DEFENDS OR PUNCHES**
- **The rule.** Every card has a defence face that matches its punch:
  - jab means **parry** (stops one straight to the head);
  - cross means **high block** (half damage from any head punch);
  - lead hook means **elbow cover** (stops one body punch);
  - rear uppercut means **shell** (halves two punches).
  
  At the end of your lead you may turn one face-up card per hand to its defence face. A turned card can't be thrown in your next lead, and you draw its replacement only after that lead. Feints have no defence face.
- **One exchange.** The defender turns the jab card (parry) and the cross card (high block). The preview shows the attacker's jab parried for 0, the cross blocked to 1 and 1 off the hook, so the hook lands for 2. The defender takes 3 instead of 7. But the defender's next lead must use the two cards left, perhaps lead body hook and body cross, so no head attack is possible.
- **The decision.** Every card is worth two things, and you see both. Defend with the card your next combination doesn't need, and keep the card that finishes it. Turn your best card only when the punches coming at you are big.
- **How it can break.** Turning cheap cards every time, so that you're always guarded. **The cap:** a defence is only as strong as its punch (a jab parries only a straight), and if you turn two cards, your next lead is two moves at most.
- **The real boxing.** A glove that is blocking can't punch at the same moment, which the two-deck setup already shows.

**5. READS** (cards created by the other boxer's habits)
- **The rule.** The defender gets a Read card when the attacker:
  - opens two leads in a row with the same move, which gives **Read: Opener**, a slip and counter against that move;
  - repeats a full combination from earlier in the bout, which gives **Read: Pattern**, so the second punch of that combination misses;
  - throws a rear power punch straight after a jab twice, which gives **Read: Telegraph**, a duck under the power punch.
  
  Reads go off by themselves, can be seen by both boxers, and last until the round ends.
- **One exchange.** In round 1 the attacker went jab, cross, lead hook twice. The defender now holds Read: Pattern and Read: Telegraph. The preview shows the jab landing for 1, the cross ducked, the hook landing for 3, and the defender's next lead getting a free counter. The attacker sees this and changes to jab, lead body hook, rear uppercut. That uses no Reads and is weaker, because two lead-hand moves in a row lose the hand-switch bonus.
- **The decision.** For the attacker, it's best combination against fresh combination. For the defender, it's which habits to bait: show a gap twice and wait.
- **How it can break.** An attacker who never repeats, or a "random" deck. **The cap:** combination bonuses for practised sequences (the third time you throw a combination it gets better) make repeating tempting. Reads end at the end of the round, so you can't build up a pile of them.
- **The real boxing.** Boxers find each other's patterns in the early rounds, and the corner says "he drops the left after the jab".

**Build first: number 1, Earned Guard Cards.** It's the owner's hint, and it uses the target lines already on every card. It also joins the two decks: the hand that doesn't throw is your guard. It needs one new row and four conditions, with no new board and no new bar. **The case against it:** number 2 costs less (one line on each card), but it gives no new moment to look forward to. Number 1 makes the defender's lead feel like it's building something. Once number 1 works, add number 5's Reads as a fifth condition.

**Published games to study**
- **[Yomi](https://www.sirlin.net/yomi)** by David Sirlin. Blocking and dodging there are a read, and a block that works draws you a card, so defence creates cards.
- **[Slay the Spire](https://www.megacrit.com/games/#slay-the-spire).** The enemy shows its next attack before you commit, so you defend with full information. Block is created by the cards you play.
- **[Unmatched](https://restorationgames.com/unmatched/)** by Restoration Games. Its "versatile" cards can be played to attack or to defend, which is number 4.
- **[Exceed](https://www.levelninetynine.com/exceed)** and **BattleCON**, both by Level 99. Range tracks and ring edges for one-on-one fighting, which is number 3.
- **[Into the Breach](https://subsetgames.com/itb.html).** Every enemy attack is shown in advance, and defence means moving out of it. That's the model for how the preview should read.

I'd build number 1 first. Defence cards are created by what you do in your own lead: a hand that throws nothing keeps its glove up, a jab that lands keeps the other boxer at range, and the defence then goes off by itself, so the attacker's preview always shows what will land.

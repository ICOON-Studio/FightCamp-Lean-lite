# Design wanted: the cover table for a southpaw opponent in Ring Switches

You are the game designer. Five rule versions failed on our measuring tool. We want YOUR cover table for the open
stance (orthodox against southpaw), built so every hold is still worth making. Plain words, short, a table first.

## The game, as it is today (both men orthodox)
Each beat a man sets three switches: his LEAD hand, his REAR hand, his FEET, and the order he throws them in.
A hand that does not punch is held HIGH or LOW and blocks one line:

| Held hand | Stops (today) | The punches on that line (health they take) |
|---|---|---|
| Lead hand high | straight head punches | the jab (1), the cross (3) |
| Lead hand low | body punches | the body jab (1), the body hook (2), the body cross (2) |
| Rear hand high | head punches from the side | the lead hook (3), the overhand (4) |
| Rear hand low | punches from below | the lead uppercut (3), the rear uppercut (4) |

Ranges: Long (jab, body jab), Mid (most punches), Inside (hooks, uppercuts, the clinch). A punching hand is out of its
guard only in the moment it punches. Two equal men who play well today: every range fought a third of the time, 16%
of bouts stopped, 2.25 knockdowns a bout, the cross thrown 3.8 times a bout, the jab lands 73%.

## What real boxing says (our researcher John, sourced)
- Against a southpaw the rear straight is thrown 25 to 40% MORE and lands as often as before (one counted study, 24
  bouts). Coaches: it comes down the middle, and the REAR hand has to stop it; that hand must move between the straight
  and the overhand, one position no longer covers both.
- An orthodox jab lands about HALF as often against a southpaw (three CompuBox notes): the lead hands are in each
  other's way; coaches teach slapping the jab down with the lead hand.
- The lead hook: coaches call it awkward in the open stance; the one count disagrees.
- The man with his lead foot outside can hit with both hands. The game does not track feet positions.

## What we tried (84 bouts each, two equal men, both in the open stance; a bar passes or fails, nine bars)
| Version | Bars | The cross, thrown a bout | The jab lands | Bouts stopped | What broke |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The page today (closed stance) | 8 of 9 | 3.8 | 73% | 16% | the jab lands too often |
| 1. The rear hand stops both rear straights (high the cross, low the body cross) | 6 of 9 (older page) | 1.5 (was 2.7) | 57% | 7% | the cross is thrown half as often |
| 2. Version 1, and a lead hand held low stops the jab too | 5 of 9 (older page) | 1.3 | 30% | 6% | the jab dies, 24% draws |
| 3. John's one rule: only the head cross moves to the rear hand held high | 5 of 9 | 2.3 | 78% | 10% | the lead hand is held high in 2% of beats: it stops only the jab |
| 4. Version 3, and the cross is a fast punch | 6 of 9 | 3.6 | 76% | 11% | the lead hook 2% and the overhand 3% of beats: dead |
| 5. Version 1, and the cross is a fast punch | 5 of 9 | 3.5 | 80% | 8% | the head jab 5% of beats, the body jab 23% |

The pattern: the rear hand held high already stops the lead hook and the overhand. Give it the cross as well and one
hold stops three big punches, so the cross is thrown LESS (the opposite of real boxing) and the lead hand held high is
left with the jab alone, so nobody holds it.

## What we want from you
1. ONE cover table for the open stance: for each of the four holds, which punches it stops. Every hold must stop at
   least one punch of 3 health or more, no hold may stop three big punches, and the rear straight must be HARDER to
   stop than today, not easier (so it is thrown more), while the jab is easier to stop.
2. For each row, the real-boxing reason in one line (a coach must be able to nod).
3. If the table needs one more thing (a punch's speed, a range, a new hold), say which ONE and why. No new switch for
   the feet.
4. What the player would SEE and read differently against the southpaw, in two lines: the game must stay as easy to
   read as it is today.
5. End with what would make this a 10 out of 10 rule for the game, most important first.

## What would a human do?
A coach would keep his rear hand at the chin for the straight and take the hooks on the lead shoulder and glove; he
would say the open stance is won with the rear hand and the lead foot.

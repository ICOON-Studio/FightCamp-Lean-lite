## Short answers
1. **How many instructions?**
   - **What trainers say.** Named trainers and a federation manual say "one or two".
     - Jim McDonnell, Gary Lockett and Adam Booth said it in Boxing News (2014) [S1].
     - Firas Zahabi said it in Fighters Only (2012) [S2].
     - The AIBA Coaches Manual says "one or two sentences" [S3].
     - All SOURCED.
   - **What recordings show.** Corners talk more than that. Coaches at the 2015 Australian Boxing Championships gave **8 ± 2 feedback statements per round** [S5]. SOURCED.
   - **The likely reconciliation.** The rule is about the one or two *things to act on*, not the number of sentences. DERIVED.
2. **What decides it?**
   - **Sources agree on the order of the minute.** Recover and breathe first, then a fitness and health check, then a short tactical message, then send the boxer out focused [S3; S4; S1; S2]. SOURCED.
   - **The tactical message is driven by three things:**
     - the opponent's habit seen in the last round [S19; S13; S10];
     - the score [S13; S21; S3];
     - the boxer's state [S2; S22].
   - **No source ranks habit, score, tiredness and damage against each other.** NOT FOUND.
3. **Wording per habit.** Real wording was found for five of the seven habits:
   - Pressure fighter: "stay off the ropes", "back into the middle".
   - Mover: "cut off the ring (do not follow)".
   - Counter-puncher: "draw the shot", "dictate the pace".
   - Big-shot loader: "move away from the power side".
   - Slow starter: "start fast".
   - Body puncher and switch-hitter: NOT FOUND.
4. **The rest round.** No sourced corner quote saying "take the round off". NOT FOUND.
   - The AIBA manual lists boxing "defensively (if winning)" in the last round [S3].
   - Numbers on work rate after a rest instruction, or on what it costs on the cards: NOT FOUND.
5. **Compliance.**
   - **The one study that measured it.** In 18 pro MMA fights, how much of the corner's advice the fighter carried out fell from a mean of 4.47 after the first break to 2.5 at the last [S6]. On that scale, 2 = one of three instructions followed and 4 = two of three. SOURCED.
   - **Retention studies (not boxing).**
     - Judo athletes kept 68.66% of a pre-bout instruction [S7].
     - Young athletes fully retained 31.6% of feedback episodes [S8].
   - **How often the corner's read is wrong.** No rate found. NOT FOUND.
6. **Corner quality.** Two studies link corner behaviour to winning, but neither shows cause.
   - **MMA [S6]:**
     - winners applied more of the corner's advice (4.14 vs 3.56);
     - losers' coaches gave more feedback (2.95 vs 2.57).
   - **Amateur boxing [S5]:** winning corners gave more praise (36% vs 18.6%).
   - **Trainer changes mid-career:** NOT FOUND. A 2026 analysis says no public dataset can separate a camp's effect from its fighters' talent [S25].

## What games get wrong about corners (only where sources back it)
1. **The corner talks straight away.** Real corners spend the first 10–40 seconds on breathing and settling [S1; S3; S4]. Lundell calls rushing in "the number-one rookie mistake" [S2].
2. **The corner gives a list.** Trainers say one or two points [S1–S3]. More ideas go with less recall [S7; S8], and with losing in MMA [S6].
3. **Every instruction is carried out in full.** In MMA, how much advice was applied fell from 4.47 to 2.5 by the final break [S6]. Youth athletes fully kept only about a third of feedback episodes [S8]. De La Hoya ignored Clancy [S20].
4. **The corner always reads the fight right.** Roach misjudged the cards [S15]. McGuigan disputed Davison's plan live [S19].
5. **The corner always tells the boxer the score.** Some corners do every round (Del Fierro [S2]). AIBA's Cuban section says not to [S3], and Kenny Porter didn't [S21].

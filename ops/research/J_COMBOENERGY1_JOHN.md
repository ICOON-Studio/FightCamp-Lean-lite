**J-COMBOENERGY1 is done, but no study measures what a 2-, 3- or 4-punch combination costs against a single punch.** I used the best stand-in instead: on the bag, a punch thrown at fast cadence costs about 1.39× one thrown slowly. That's DERIVED from [Arseneau 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21217532/), where oxygen use was 24.7, 30.4 and 38.3 ml/kg/min at 60, 120 and 180 punches per minute. Punching is a small part of the total: at about 18 punches a minute it adds only about 5% of the cost of sparring.

**Output across rounds** comes from the women's 2023 world finals, 12 bouts ([Martusciello 2025a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452553/), MEASURED):
- **Winners** threw 19.3, 16.8 and 18.1 punches per minute in rounds 1–3. Their rear-hand punches fell 19% in round 2, while the jab barely moved, so tired winners drop power shots first.
- **Accuracy** stayed flat for winners (0.39, 0.38, 0.39), and losers actually improved slightly.
- **Combinations** made up 46.6% of winners' actions, but only as a whole-bout figure. No one has published combination share by round.
- **Exchanges** in the 81+ kg final shortened by 31% from round 1 to round 3 ([Martusciello 2025b](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12197034/)). In the 52 kg final they got longer.
- **Elite men** ([Dunn 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5747423/)) threw a flat number of punches per round, but clinch time rose 78%, time with the guard down rose 41%, and bouncing fell 23%.

**Tiredness and technique:**
- **Reaction time** slowed about 18% after round 3, but only for boxers not yet peaked for competition ([Hukkanen 2017](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001885)).
- **Punch force** didn't fall across rounds. [Smith 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3863921/) measured 1265 N in round 1 and 1314 N in round 4, and Hukkanen saw force rise.
- **Punch speed and travel time** didn't change in any study we found.
- **The time between punches in a combination** under fatigue hasn't been published.

**Recovery in the 60 s break** ([Davis 2014](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0006), MEASURED unless marked):
- **Heart rate** drops only about 13 bpm in each break, and each round runs about 7 bpm higher than the last (166, 173, 174).
- **Lactate** keeps climbing (6.7, 8.6, 9.5 mmol/L), so the break doesn't clear it.
- **Muscles' fast energy store:** about half of what's used up refills in 60 s. That's DERIVED from [Bogdanis 1995](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1157744/), which gives a half-time of 56.6 s.
- **Fitness:** fitter athletes recover faster, but there's no boxing number for how much.

**Proposed rule for a 100-point energy tank:**
- **Targets (ESTIMATE):** at finals pace she ends rounds at about 68, 46 and 29, and starts rounds 2 and 3 at about 78 and 61.
- **Drain (ESTIMATE unless marked):**
  - Moving and guarding: 0.10 points per second.
  - A single punch: 0.22 points.
  - A combination of n punches: 0.22 × [1 + 1.39 × (n−1)], where 1.39 is DERIVED. That gives 0.53, 0.83 and 1.14 points for 2, 3 and 4 punches.
  - Rear-hand punches and hooks cost 1.1× as much.
- **Recovery (ESTIMATE):** 60% of what she spends comes back quickly and 40% slowly.
  - Each break refills 52% of the quick part (DERIVED).
  - The slow part never refills during the fight.
  - A fitness factor from 0.8 to 1.2 scales recovery.

In plain words, between energy 70 and 30 she throws about 10% fewer punches (DERIVED). Her combinations get about 30% shorter (ESTIMATE), and she throws 19% fewer power shots (DERIVED).

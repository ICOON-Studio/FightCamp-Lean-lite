# J_TELLS1: what a boxer reads before a punch

This is research only. Every page was opened on 25 Nov 2026 (Asia/Nicosia) unless another date is given.

How each claim is tagged:
- **SOURCED:** a measured study or count.
- **COACH:** a coach's teaching.
- **COMMENTATOR:** a commentator's or analyst's claim.
- **MEMORY:** my own recall, not checked.
- **DERIVED:** my own arithmetic on sourced numbers.
- **CHECK:** needs a second look.
- **NOT FOUND:** searched for, and nothing found.

"Side view" means how well a cue shows to a camera at 90° to the line between the two boxers. That's my own judgement from the body part involved, because no study rates it (NOT FOUND).

**The fact behind everything else: watching the fist is too late.**
- **How fast the punch lands:** once the fist leaves the guard, an experienced boxer's punch lands in about 70–120 ms. Measured on 31 boxers, the throwing part took 117 ms for the jab, 111 for the cross, 91 for the rear hook and 71 for the uppercut [S2] (SOURCED).
- **How fast people react:** in a karate study of 13 people [S8] (SOURCED):
  - a simple reaction to a dot on a screen took 231–252 ms;
  - deciding from video whether an attack was going high or to the middle took 552 ms for athletes and 657 ms for novices;
  - the attacks themselves lasted 430–660 ms.
- **What follows (DERIVED):** a defender who waits for the fist is too late. Every read that works comes from what moves *before* the fist.

## Q1. The tells for each punch

- **Jab.**
  - COACH: "lead shoulder rises, small step in, front heel lifts" [S20].
  - COACH: a good jab has "no big wind-up, no obvious weight shift… The less movement there is before the punch, the harder it is to see coming" [S25].
  - Measured: the jab's throwing part takes 117 ms [S2]. A lead time for the jab is NOT FOUND.
- **Cross (rear straight).**
  - SOURCED, how it works:
    - the punch starts in the legs and runs through the hip, the shoulder and the fist [S3];
    - in a cross, the shoulder, hip, elbow and fist reach top speed one after another [S1];
    - the rear straight uses more body turn than the lead [S4].
  - COACH: "rear hip turns, rear heel lifts, chin tucks" [S20], and "Shoulder loading: the rear shoulder pulling back slightly before a cross or overhand" [S21].
- **Lead hook.**
  - SOURCED: hooks have a bigger wind-up than straights, and take longer from wind-up to contact (477 against 357 ms) [S1].
  - COACH: the lead hand dips or the lead shoulder opens, and weight goes onto the lead leg [S20, S21].
- **Rear hook and overhand.**
  - SOURCED: at the start, the jab, cross and rear hook gloves all drop, and the rear hook's drops the most [S1].
  - COACH: the rear shoulder pulls back, and a small step out to the side with the lead foot sets up the angle for the hook or overhand [S21].
  - A measurement for the overhand alone is NOT FOUND.
- **Uppercuts.**
  - COACH: the knees dip, the shoulder drops and the elbow leaves the body [S20].
  - Measured: the throwing part is 71 ms, the shortest of the four punches [S2].
- **Body punches.**
  - COACH: the head drops a level as the knees bend [S23].
  - COACH: looking down at the body is "the #1 most obvious noob mistake" [S23], and the eyes dropping gives away a body shot [S21].
  - No counted evidence (NOT FOUND).
- **Step in and step back.**
  - SOURCED, from karate: the last step that closes the distance was the cue used most [S12].
  - COACH: a step in, or a heel lifting [S20].
  - A tell for the *step back* is NOT FOUND, in coaching or in studies.
- **A single punch or a combination.**
  - SOURCED: punches inside a combination showed less drop at the start and less wind-up [S1].
  - DERIVED from [S1]: a visible wind-up marks a single big punch, while a fast combination hides it.

## Q2. How reliable the reads are (see Table B)

- **Across combat sports, experts read attacks better than novices:** 83.3% against 68.5%, pooled [S9] (SOURCED). For boxing on its own, the difference was not significant, but that rests on only 2 studies [S9].
- **The cleanest single number comes from karate:** 96% against 80% on a two-way call from the first frames of the attack [S8] (SOURCED).
- **The one boxing study with numbers:**
  - Both groups scored about 20% when the clip stopped 80 ms before the action started. Their hit rate rose to 64–75% once the clip ran to 40 ms before the start [S10] (SOURCED).
  - What follows (DERIVED): almost nothing can be read before the move itself begins.
- **Where experts look:** at the middle of the body (head, chest and solar plexus), not at the punching hand [S13, S16, and S11 citing Ripoll] (SOURCED).
- **Left-handed opponents are harder to read.** That was shown in volleyball and hasn't been tested in boxing [S17] (SOURCED, another sport). It links to the open-stance memo J_SOUTHPAW1.

## Q3. What a guard position gives away (COACH only)

- **Lead hand dropping on the jab:** "you are leaving yourself open to getting counterpunched… you are exposing your face" [S24]. Dropping it before punching "is telegraphing" [S25].
- **Rear hand off the chin when jabbing:** it "leaves you open to a counter hook from your opponent" [S24]. "Your rear hand should be glued to your chin. Many beginners drop it or let it float away" [S25].
- **Hands low between exchanges** marks a tiring boxer: "A fatigued fighter starts carrying their hands lower between exchanges… Their combinations shorten" [S21] (COACH, CHECK, unsigned page).
- **What a low guard says about the *next* punch:** NOT FOUND, apart from the lead hand dipping before a hook [S21].

## Q4. Habits and how predictable a boxer is

- **Share taken by the favourite punch, counted:** in one CompuBox bout, the jab was 70.6% and 65.4% of the two boxers' punches [S27] (SOURCED, a single bout). Our G99 puts jabs at 40–44% of punches thrown on average.
- **Amateur counts (SOURCED):**
  - Novice amateurs (32 boxers): winners threw more combinations than losers (44.3 against 28.8 in rounds 1 and 3), and more block-and-counter combinations (2.8 against 0.1) [S28].
  - Olympic semi-finals and finals (29 bouts): about 20 punches and 2.5 defensive moves a minute [S29].
  - World Championships after the 2013 rule changes (50 bouts): winners threw more straight punches [S30].
  - Amateurs (66 boxers): two-punch combinations were the most common [S31]. I only read the abstract, so the shares are NOT FOUND.
- **Repetition can be learned:**
  - SOURCED: karate experts caught an attack repeated every fourth action after its fifth repeat [S14].
  - SOURCED: boxers improved 7.89% after watching an opponent, but said they went on the *movement*, not the *favourite punch* [S11].
  - COACH: coaching drills teach the same idea: repeat a pattern five times, then counter on the sixth. "Nobody throws the same pattern consciously. But under pressure, everyone defaults to habits" [S26].
- **How often a combination repeats in competition:** NOT FOUND.
- **Any analysis of punch sequences in competition** (which punch follows which): NOT FOUND.
- **Whether a tired boxer is easier to read:** NOT FOUND as a measurement. Related findings (SOURCED):
  - physical tiredness cut punch force by 4.26% [S7], but readability wasn't tested;
  - in the 2023 IBA women's finals, missed body punches rose in round 3 for both sides, which the authors read as possible tiredness [S32] (the J50 source);
  - mental tiredness in the *boxer doing the reading* hurt performance [S10].

## Q5. Feints

- **What a feint is for (COACH):**
  - The amateur coaches' manual calls it "Hiding boxer's intention by deceptive action" and says it's used "To make opponent to take any attack action that will create an opening for the counter-attack". It lists head, eye, arm, body (shoulder) and leg (knee dip) feints [S19].
  - A coaching plan says "A feint is a lie you tell with your body", and that a feint "loses its value if the follow-up is predictable" [S33].
- **How often feints are used:** NOT FOUND. A reliable template for scoring amateur bouts defines four kinds of feint [S34], but I only read its abstract, so there are no counts.
- **Whether feints work (SOURCED):**
  - **A boxing-specific test** (20 boxers and 20 non-boxers): reactions were slower to a punch on the same side as a fake punch thrown 600 ms earlier. More years of training went with a *bigger* effect: "even athletes trained to avoid deception can be as susceptible as novices if the timing of the feint is right" [S18].
  - **VR boxing** (18 experts): the first punch after a *foot* feint was defended *better* than after an arm feint or no feint [S15]. So the foot feint seems to have helped the defender (CHECK).
  - **Fencing** (another sport, 24 elite fencers): moving to defend took 0.353 s. How fast the feint moved at the edge of vision didn't change when elite fencers started to defend [S35].
  - **A review:** whether fakes reliably fool experts isn't settled [S36].

## Q6. Styles

No study and no named coach gives *different cues for reading* a pressure fighter, a range jabber and a body puncher (NOT FOUND). What does exist:
- **Stance gives away the style:**
  - "A wide, square stance usually means a brawler who wants to trade. A narrow, bladed stance with the lead shoulder high suggests a counter-puncher."
  - "Flat-footed fighters telegraph everything through weight shifts", while a fighter up on the toes is harder to read [S21] (COACH, CHECK, unsigned page).
- **Body punchers** give away the level change and the eyes dropping [S23] (COACH).
- **The amateur coaches' manual** gives *tactics* against tall, short, jab-heavy, heavy-punching, counter-punching and opposite-stance opponents, not cues for reading them [S19] (COACH).
- **Martial arts (another sport):** kung fu fighters, who attack with arms and legs, fix their eyes higher than taekwondo fighters do [S37] (SOURCED).
